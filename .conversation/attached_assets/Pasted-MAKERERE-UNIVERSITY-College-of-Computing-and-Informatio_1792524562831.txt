1. Introduction
This deliverable follows Approach 2: a real problem in our environment. The problem is
short-distance travel around Makerere University Main Campus, Kikoni and Wandegeya.
Walking is cheap but slow and tiring, especially in rain. Boda-bodas are faster, but fares are
high for frequent short trips and rise at peak hours and in bad weather. A digital campus bike
service once operated at Makerere but has closed, so no affordable alternative is currently
available.
EasyRide is a proposed community bicycle-rental service with a web-based interface for two
user groups: customers (students, staff, workers, visitors) and operators (people who manage
bicycles, rentals, returns, payments and maintenance). As required, the project excludes native
apps, GPS, sensors, smart locks and robots. Any availability shown will therefore be the most
recently recorded status, not live tracking.
1.1 Background: The Previous Campus Bike Service
Makerere previously had a digital bike-sharing service. Users signed up through an app,
obtained a bicycle at one of several stations across campus, and ended their trip by returning it
to a station. A former operator of the service (OP2) told us that it worked digitally and solved
some travel challenges, but closed because of high operational costs and relatively low returns.
The same operator now runs the service in other environments that already have some basic
conditions in place.
This history matters for design: students still need an affordable short-distance option, and a
working interface alone did not keep the service going. Our problem identification therefore
considers both what users need and what operators need to keep the service sustainable.
1.2 Scope
The study is limited to Makerere and its surroundings. Because the previous operator now
serves other places, we treat stations, prices and locations as configurable information in the
interface, so the same design could later be reused elsewhere. Wider expansion is outside the
scope of this project.
Aim of the study: to understand how people currently make short trips and how rentals are
managed, and to turn the evidence into a clear design problem.

2.2 Methods
• Semi-structured interviews guided by a topic guide (Section 3), starting with recent real
experiences and using follow-ups such as “What happened next?”
• Contextual observation of P2 searching for a bicycle (Section 5).
• Thematic analysis: notes were reviewed after each session, and repeated difficulties
were grouped into themes and needs (Section 6).
2.3 Ethical and Sensitivity Measures
• Consent: each participant was told the academic purpose, that taking part is voluntary,
that they may skip any question or stop at any time, and that only written notes would be
taken.
• Privacy: names, contacts, faces and identifying details are excluded. Photos or recordings
are used only with permission.
• Sensitive topics: money, safety incidents and work circumstances were discussed
neutrally and optionally. No one was asked about income or family circumstances; only
transport spending was discussed.
• Respect for the extreme user: EU1’s situation is described as a design constraint, not
as hardship or a deficiency.
• Business information: the former operator’s organisation, current locations and any
commercial details are not named in this report.
• Data handling: notes are stored securely and used only for this coursework.
2.4 Limitations
The sample is small and drawn from one area, so the findings give design insight and are not
statistically representative. The history of the previous service comes from one former
operator’s account and has not been independently verified.

3. Interview Guide
Opening statement (read to every participant): “We are Makerere students doing a class
project on short-distance travel and bicycle rental. Taking part is voluntary. You may skip any
question or stop at any time. We will take written notes only, and your name will not be used.
May we continue?”
3.1 Potential Customers
1. Tell us about your most recent short trip around campus, Kikoni or Wandegeya.
2. How did you travel, and why did you choose that option?
3. What was the hardest part of the trip (cost, time, weather, other)?
4. Roughly how much did the trip cost? (optional)
5. Have you rented a bicycle before? How did you find and choose it?
6. What would you need to know before renting (price, condition, location)?
7. What did you do when your preferred option was not available?
8. What would make short trips easier for you?
3.2 Operator
1. How do you record bicycles and their status?
2. How do you know which bicycles are available, rented or faulty?
3. What do you record at rental and return, including payments?
4. What happens when many customers arrive at once?
5. How are faults reported, and how do you stop a faulty bicycle being issued?
6. What would make your work more reliable?
3.3 Extreme User (added to 3.1)
1. Walk us through a typical working evening.
2. What happens when a trip takes longer or costs more than planned?
3. What would save you the most time on a trip?
3.4 Former Operator (service history)
1. How did the previous campus bike service work from a user’s point of view?
2. What worked well for users, and what did not?
3. What led to the service closing?
4. What conditions do you need in place when starting the service in a new place?

7. Problem Identification
Core problem. The problem is not the lack of a website. It is the lack of a clear, joined-up
process that lets customers make confident rental decisions and lets operators keep accurate
records.
Problem statement. People making short trips around Makerere University need an
affordable, dependable way to locate, evaluate, rent and return bicycles, but information on
pickup points, availability, price, condition and return confirmation is scattered or missing.
Operators need a reliable way to record bicycles, rentals, payments and faults, because manual
methods cause omissions, billing disputes and the reissue of faulty bicycles. The previous
digital campus service also closed because of operating costs and low returns, so the design
must help operators keep the service sustainable.
Point of view. EU1, an evening student and on-campus document courier, needs a fast,
dependable and affordable way to find, rent and return a bicycle because they make up to 12
time-sensitive trips in about 3 hours on a daily transport budget of UGX 1,500.
Design challenge (HMW). How might we help people making short trips confidently locate,
evaluate, rent and return a bicycle, while helping operators keep accurate availability, rental,
payment and maintenance records and monitor usage, through a web interface that can be set
up for different locations?
Constraints. The solution must be web-based; serve customers and operators; need no sensors
or special hardware; separate recorded from physically verified availability; treat stations,
prices and locations as configurable so it can be reused elsewhere; stay simple under time
pressure; and protect personal and payment information.
8. Conclusion
Interviews and observation with seven participants, including one extreme user and a former
operator of the previous campus bike service, show that the main difficulties are unpredictable
cost, unknown availability, unclear bicycle condition, fragmented operator records and the
difficulty of sustaining the service. These needs and the problem statement will guide persona
development, How Might We questions, ideation and storyboarding in the next deliverable.
